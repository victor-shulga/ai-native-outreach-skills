# The method behind the canvas

This file explains how the copywriting canvas works for a service business and why each step is
there. It is an adaptation for agencies and consultancies of the SaaS homepage messaging framework
by two product-marketing consultants; see Credits in `SKILL.md`. The wording, the
service-business examples and the site-structure step are Victor Shulga's.

## Why service homepages go vague

Everyone inside the company wants something different from the homepage. The founders want every
service and every industry listed. Delivery wants the technology stack on display. Sales wants
the commercial terms. HR wants the culture. Each request is reasonable; together they produce a
page that says a little about everything and nothing a buyer can act on.

The fix is a single test. A visitor from the target market should be able to say, quickly:
1. Which services does this company sell?
2. How does working with them run?
3. What exactly gets delivered?
4. In what situation would I hire them?
5. How does that move forward the thing I am working on right now?

Questions 1 and 3 matter more for services than for software, because a service has no product
screenshot to show. The deliverable has to be named in words.

## Step 1: The minimum site a service company needs

The homepage routes people; it does not carry the whole sale. Before rewriting it, check that the
pages it routes to exist:

1. **Homepage**: value proposition, capabilities, the problems you solve, client logos.
2. **Case studies**: filterable by industry, geography, service, stack.
3. **Company page**: team, how you work, why choose you, awards and certifications.
4. **Service pages**: one per service, industry or geography, each with a summary, benefits,
   problems fixed, proof and a CTA. These do most of the SEO work.
5. **Pricing or engagement models**: pricing backed by proof, an FAQ that answers objections, the
   cost of doing nothing.
6. **Resources**, matched to awareness: for people who do not yet see the problem (webinars, long
   guides, symptom lists), for people who see it (templates, quizzes, scorecards), and for people
   comparing solutions (comparisons, ROI calculators).

## Step 2: Choose the audience

Lay out candidate segments on three axes: **company type × person or department × situation**.

- Messaging tied to a persona ("agency owner") or a company type ("custom software firms") is
  **vertical**.
- Messaging tied to a situation that many company types share is **horizontal**.
- For a high-ticket service with a long sales cycle and a modest marketing budget, a horizontal
  message rarely gets enough reach to work. Start vertical. (The original framework suggests a budget threshold for
  this; treat any such number as a rule of thumb, not data.)
- A situation can be a process the buyer is running or an outcome they are chasing. Either way,
  the buyer must be spending effort on it now.

To choose between audience options, ask:
- Where do we want growth in the next 12 months?
- Which group can our current sales and marketing actually reach?
- Are we capturing demand that already exists, or trying to create it?

## Step 3: Write for the champion, in terms of activities

The executive who signs has authority but usually sits too far from the problem to care about the
details of your service. A B2B service deal takes months, so write for the **champion**: the
person close enough to the problem to feel its cost, with the time and motive to persuade
colleagues, handle procurement and keep the deal moving. The champion is often, but not always,
the person who will work with you day to day.

Describe what the champion is **doing**, not what they want in the abstract. "Grow revenue" and
"save time" are outcomes and too high-level to write against. Break them into activities:
- "Stay compliant" becomes: run staff reviews, standardise procedures, prepare audit evidence.
- "Ship more projects" becomes: scope incoming work, staff the team, check drawings before issue.

Keep activities **tool-agnostic**: describe the task, not the software used to do it. Then strike
out every activity your service does not touch, and keep detailing what remains until it reads as
slightly too specific. That is the level at which a buyer recognises themselves.

## Step 4: The left half of the canvas

For one primary use case, write down:
- **Current way**: how the champion handles the situation today (in-house team, freelancers,
  another vendor, doing nothing).
- **Limitations**: neutral facts about that current way. A limitation is not a complaint; it is a
  property ("the in-house team is fully booked until Q3").
- **Problems**: what each limitation costs the champion ("so new projects slip and the client
  starts asking questions").

Read the canvas row by row, then column by column, the way you would read a user-story map.

## Step 5: The right half of the canvas

- **Capabilities**: what the client can do once they work with you.
  Example: "Generate pipeline and close more deals."
- **Features**: the concrete mechanics that make the capability real.
  Example: "With SOPs, frameworks, templates and an operator who has hit these targets before."
- **Benefits**: the result of using the capability.
  Example: "More new revenue from leads that match your ICP."

Two links hold the canvas together:
- each **capability improves a limitation** from the left half;
- each **benefit removes a problem** from the left half.

If a capability does not answer a limitation, it does not belong on the homepage.

## Step 6: From canvas to copy

The blocks that carry a service homepage:
- **Hero**: the top-row message plus the key capabilities, the service category the market
  already uses, and a trust line.
- **Problem section**: built from current way, limitations and problems. It tells the right
  visitor they are in the right place and tells the wrong visitor to leave.
- **Solution**: three arguments, each a capability with its feature and benefit, leading to one
  next step (a form or a booking link).

The lower half depends on the company and mostly routes visitors onward: services (to service
pages), industries, testimonials, why work with us, segmentation by persona or by company type,
an ROI block, and a final calendar or form.
