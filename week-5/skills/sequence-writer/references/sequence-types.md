# The three anchors

Every cold sequence rests on one of three anchors. Do not mix types in one
campaign — you will not be able to tell which one worked.

| Type | Rests on | Accounts | Urgency |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Signal | an event with a date | 50–300 / month | high, the signal expires |
| B. Data-point | a measurable state | hundreds–thousands | medium |
| C. Nothing | an insight about the segment | the rest of the base | none, you create it |

---

## Type A — signal

A signal is the external symptom of the problem your service solves. It has a date.

Examples: raised a round · hired a CTO or VP Engineering · opened 5 engineering
roles in a quarter · announced a migration off an old stack or a rewrite ·
announced a release, a new direction, a partnership · entered a new market or
opened an office · ended a contract with a vendor · their decision maker
complained about a problem publicly.

### Two questions before you use it

1. What problem does this person have **now that this event happened**?
2. Does your service solve **that exact** problem?

"Roughly" on question 2 means the signal is not yours. A signal with no link to
your problem turns the email into "hi, I saw your news, let's talk".

### Expiry windows

| Signal | Window |
|---|---|
| New person in a role | 14–90 days (best in the first 30) |
| Funding round | 30–90 days |
| Open roles | 7–30 days, while they are open |
| Won tender / contract | 7–21 days |
| Post or comment by the decision maker | 24–72 hours |
| Release, announcement | 7–14 days |

Writing about a three-month-old event as news proves the list is old, not that
you did your homework.

### Sequence shape

The whole sequence rests on the one signal. Do not hunt for a new pretext in
every email.

1. Signal, hypothesis about the problem, proof, question
2. The same event from another angle: a different consequence of it
3. Asset: "put together two pages on how others handle this, want it?"
4. Proof: one case from the same sub-vertical, one number
5. Close: "looks like the timing is off, I'll leave it there"

### Example

> Subject: series b hiring
>
> Mark, saw you closed the $18M round on July 28 and opened five backend roles the same week.
>
> Five senior hires in this market usually take two quarters, and the roadmap you raised on assumes they start now.
>
> A payments team at your stage put an outside squad on the integrations track in 10 days and kept their own hires for the core product.
>
> Worth a look at how they split it?

## Type B — data-point

No event. But there is a measurable, verifiable fact about the state of the company.

Examples: 8 engineers carrying two product tracks, quarterly releases · 40% of
roles open for 90+ days · a stack on Rails 5 and Node 14, last public commit six
months ago · a mobile app not updated in 8 months while it takes payments · 70%
of revenue from one client · a job ad asking for ten technologies at a mid-level
rate · a status page showing weekly incidents · no page for a service they
clearly sell.

A data-point does not expire. It also does not create urgency on its own — the
urgency has to be added.

### A data-point with no comparison communicates nothing

This is the whole type. "You have 8 engineers" is a row from a database.

"8 engineers on two product tracks, neither of them owned by a separate team,
so the mobile release waits on backend" reads like a thought worth finishing.

**Formula: data-point + what you compare it to + what that usually means.**

Compare against: the industry norm · competitors they know · their own number a
year ago · what it looks like at your clients after the work.

### Where data-points come from

Company site (service pages, portfolio, pricing, team) · job ads (how many, how
long open, which tools) · LinkedIn headcount dynamics · tech stack · tender and
permit registries · financial filings if public · site traffic and visibility.

### Sequence shape

1. Data-point, comparison, what it means, question
2. A second data-point from the same slice: reinforces the first, does not replace it
3. Asset carrying the same metric for the industry
4. Proof: the same metric at a client after the work
5. Close

### Two or three contacts, not a fourth email

A persona swap is never a step inside a sequence. The person you write to for
the first time would receive an email written as a fourth touch.

Rule: **2–3 contacts per company, in parallel, 5 days apart, from different
mailboxes.** Each gets a full sequence and their own anchor for their role. They
are never mentioned to each other.

The reason is not coverage. With one contact you cannot tell "wrong person" from
"wrong company", and you will kill a working segment by mistake.

### Example

> Subject: react native roles
>
> Sarah, you've had 6 engineering roles open for 90+ days, three of them senior React Native.
>
> Teams that size usually end up pulling their own seniors into interviews, and the release slips a sprint or two per quarter.
>
> A US fintech moved the mobile track to an outside squad of 3 and shipped the release while their own hiring caught up.
>
> Can I send the split they used?

## Type C — when there is nothing

The most common case and the least documented one: in most bases 60–80% of
accounts have neither a fresh signal nor a data-point. Three exits work.

### Exit 1 — a segment insight instead of personalisation

Personalise to a segment cut narrow enough to be recognised.

Bad: *"Working with construction companies…"*

Good: *"Writing to Series A fintechs in the US shipping on one in-house team.
You're the fourth this month whose mobile roadmap is waiting on a single hire."*

Test: **if your first line could be pasted unchanged into an email to their
competitor, the segment is too wide.**

### Exit 2 — an open hypothesis

You do not claim to know their situation. You place the bet out loud and give
them the right to refute it.

> Realize I may be off here, but teams your size usually hit the wall when the roadmap needs two tracks and hiring only fills one.

This removes the strongest objection to a cold email — "you know nothing about
us" — because you admitted it first.

### Exit 3 — proof instead of observation

If there is nothing to say about them, give something useful before asking for
anything. The CTA leads to the material. The call is not mentioned.

> Put together a breakdown of how 12 Series A teams split the roadmap between in-house and an outside squad. 2 pages, no pitch. Want it?

### What not to do in type C

Invented personalisation ("saw your site, impressed by your approach") ·
`{{industry}}` dropped into an empty construction · "I hope this finds you well"
to fill the first line · starting with yourself because there is nothing else.

### Measuring type C

Measure it as its own campaign. Blended with signal campaigns, the signal ones
pull the average up and you never see that the segment track produces nothing.

If type C covers 100% of your base, the problem is not the copy. It is the base.
Under 10% of accounts carrying any signal = rebuild the base.
