# The LinkedIn track

Same sequence logic, different format, harder limits.

## The invite note — the rule changed

"An empty invite wins" held for years. The 2025–2026 data says it is half true.

| | Accept | Replies |
|---|---|---|
| Invite with a note | 25.3% | 8.2% |
| Empty invite | 27.6% | 5.3% |

Belkins, 15.1M touches in 2025; three other independent datasets point the same
way. A note costs 0–4 points of accept rate and returns 1.5–1.9x more replies.

**Write the note, and stop treating accept rate as the headline metric.** Whoever
optimises accept deletes the note and halves their replies. Watch replies per
invite sent, and meetings per thousand.

Note length: **120–180 characters**. A full 300 loses — it reads as a pitch. A
note with a real pretext gets 50–60% accept against 15–25% for a generic one.

One trap: on a free account only a handful of notes are available per month,
after which invites go out empty. This is a practitioner observation, not a
published LinkedIn number — check it on your own account before planning.

## First message after the accept

**150–300 characters.** This is a measured curve, not a style preference:
50–100 characters give ~16% replies, 150–300 give ~30%, 800+ fall back to 21%.
Too short loses the same way too long does.

Structure: one observation about them → one concrete thought → one easy question.
No link, no calendar, no pitch.

Honest denominator: **~80% of people who accept an invite never reply at all.**
That is the channel, not your copy.

## Three touches, not four

Confirmed twice on separate samples:

| Touch | What it adds |
|---|---|
| First follow-up | zero. On one sample, −0.6% against writing nothing |
| Second follow-up | +4 points. The only one that actually works |
| Third and beyond | about +1 point |

So: **anchor → 3–5 day pause → follow-up that carries something new → 3–5 day
pause → close.** The first follow-up is never a reminder; if it carries no value
it is empty.

Replies by message number: first ~15%, second 7.5%, third 4.5%, fourth 1.7%.

## What goes in each step

| Step | Type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Invite | note, 120–180 chars | the pretext and nothing else. Nothing is sold |
| After accept | anchor | observation, thought, question. Up to 300 chars |
| +3–5 days | asset or number | something absent from the first: one client number, or a two-page breakdown |
| +3–5 days | close | a direct question or an exit. No guilt |

## Worked examples — custom development

**Invite note** (signal, 148 chars):

> Mark, saw the round close on the 28th and five backend roles opened the same week. Curious how you're splitting the roadmap while hiring catches up.

**First message after accept:**

> Thanks for connecting, Mark. Straight to why I reached out: teams that raise and open five roles at once usually end up running the roadmap on the people they already have, and the new track waits two quarters for hires.
>
> How are you covering the integrations work in the meantime, in-house or outside?

**Second touch, day 3–5:**

> One number instead of a nudge: a payments team at your stage put an outside squad on integrations in 10 days and kept their own hires for the core product. Their release date didn't move.
>
> Want the two-page breakdown of how they split it?

**Close:**

> Last note from me, Mark. I'll assume the timing's off and leave it there.
>
> If it helps later, the offer stands async: send one track from the roadmap and I'll mark up where teams your size usually lose the sprint. No call needed.

## Outstaffing vs dedicated team — different messages

Decide what you are selling before writing. It changes both the closing question
and the objection you will get back.

| | Outstaffing | Dedicated team |
|---|---|---|
| What you close | a role they cannot hire for | a track sitting in the queue |
| Whose process | theirs: their PM, standups, board | ours: own team, own rhythm, reporting outward |
| Unit of conversation | a person and a rate | a scope and a date |
| Closing question | who is carrying this work now | is this track in the quarter's plan or parked |
| Main objection | "we only hire in-house" | "we won't hand a whole track to strangers" |

### Outstaffing — roles left hanging

**Note** (144 chars):

> Sarah, saw the three senior Node roles have been open since May. Curious whether you're holding the roadmap for them or covering it another way.

**After accept** (263 chars):

> Thanks for connecting, Sarah. When senior roles sit open past a quarter the work doesn't wait. It lands on the two people you can least afford to slow down. We put vetted engineers into your own process and stack, usually live in two weeks. Who's covering it now?

**Second touch:**

> One number instead of a nudge: a US health-tech team filled two backend seats in 11 days and kept the release date they'd already promised the board.
>
> Want the two-page breakdown of how they onboarded them?

**Close:**

> Last note from me, Sarah. I'll take it the timing is off.
>
> If hiring stalls again, send me the role and I'll tell you straight whether an outside engineer or a hire is the cheaper answer for it. No call needed.

### Dedicated team — the second track

**Note** (146 chars):

> Mark, saw the mobile app hasn't shipped since January while the web side moved twice. Curious how you're planning to run both tracks next quarter.

**After accept** (252 chars):

> Thanks for connecting, Mark. Two tracks on one team usually means the quieter one waits, and mobile is the one that waits. A dedicated squad of three runs the second track end to end: own standups, your PM. Is mobile on the plan this quarter or parked?

**Second touch:**

> Concrete: a payments client kept their in-house team on the core product and gave us the mobile track. First release in nine weeks, no new hires.
>
> Want the split they used?

**Close:**

> Last one from me, Mark. If the second track is parked until you hire, that's a fair answer.
>
> Send me the roadmap slice when it unparks and I'll tell you what a squad costs against a hire.

### Frozen headcount — routed through project budget

Works specifically in custom development: they cannot hire, but they can spend.

**Note** (131 chars):

> Anna, saw eng headcount is flat since January while the roadmap doubled. Curious how you're covering the gap without new headcount.

**After accept** (265 chars):

> Thanks for connecting, Anna. Flat headcount plus a bigger roadmap usually gets solved by asking the same people for more sprints. Contractors sit outside headcount and come off the project budget, a different line entirely. How are you covering the extra scope now?

**Second touch:**

> One number: a UK SaaS team added four engineers on the project budget while headcount stayed frozen, and shipped the integrations release a quarter early.
>
> Want the breakdown of how they booked it?

**Close:**

> Last note, Anna. If the answer is "we wait for the freeze to lift", that's fair.
>
> Ping me when it does and I'll send the ramp plan we use, so the first month isn't onboarding.

## Referrals — ask directly

The only published A/B on this: the hedged version ("Or am I way off?") produced
20.9% positive replies, the direct version 30%. The hedge hands people a
convenient excuse to disqualify themselves.

So: **"Would it make more sense to talk to {name} about this?"** with the name
already found. Not "who handles this on your side?".

## Voice notes and video

No dataset with a disclosed sample has measured either on LinkedIn. The only
figure from a source that named its sample size: +10–15% at a length under 30
seconds. Everything else ("2–3x") is published without a sample or a control.

Technical limits: voice note up to 60 seconds, recorded on mobile only, first
degree only, does not work in InMail, cannot be reviewed before sending.

Sources name accent and intelligibility as the main failure mode. A team sending
45 seconds of audio to a US or UK decision maker without a transcript loses to
the same message in text. Keep voice for the founder, on a warm contact, late in
the conversation.

## InMail

Paid InMail does not pay off here: a cold Open InMail returns 7.5% replies
against 7.9% for an ordinary cold invite with a note. You are paying credits for
the same result. Sales Navigator gives 50 credits a month and refunds the credit
if the person replies within 90 days — so InMail makes sense only where a reply
is close to certain.

## Limits and safety

- **10–19 invites per day per profile** produce the highest accept rate, 32.0%. At 20–29/day it drops to 26.7%. Five senders at 15–20 invites beat one account run at maximum, on both risk and result
- planned ceiling ~100 invites per week per profile. Vendors promising 200–300 are selling a tool
- accept below 20% means broken targeting or a broken profile. A second channel does not cure it
- withdraw unaccepted invites — but LinkedIn blocks a new invite to that person for about three weeks afterwards, so do not withdraw people you plan to return to soon
- January is a dead month: 21.45% accept, a third below the annual average. Do not launch a first campaign in January
- LinkedIn publishes no numeric limits. Everything except InMail credits and the 30,000-connection ceiling is practitioner observation
- automation of any kind violates the terms of use. Nobody has demonstrated a risk difference between cloud services and browser extensions: LinkedIn detects session behaviour, not where the session comes from

## Profile

The profile is a threshold, not a lever. The accept-rate difference between a
C-level sender and a rank-and-file one is 3.1 points; between recipient
industries it is 22 points. Who you write to matters about seven times more than
who you are.

In practice: fix the founder's profile in one evening and go back to working the
list. Headline = audience plus result, not a job title. Featured carries three
things: a post, the site, one hard asset. A banner with a sentence readable on a
phone.

What actually moves the number: **a manual comment on their post a day before the
invite lifts accept from 27% to 44%.** Automatic likes and profile views do the
opposite — campaigns with auto-warmup show 22% accept against 29.6% for plain ones.

## No longer works

- "I see we're both in group X" — LinkedIn removed the shared group as a connect reason
- auto-likes and auto-views as warmup: measured, negative
- pitching immediately after the accept
- "got 15 minutes?" in the first message
- spintax: it now has its own statistical fingerprint
- AI openers like "I loved your post about X": the difference between AI-written and ordinary messages was 0.3 points on a sample of 15.1M touches
